Why most HIV exposures never become infections

Jad AlmajnouniIndependent project, 2026Code on GitHubPython · Gillespie, SDEs

A deterministic model says any virus with a reproduction number above one always takes off. Real infections start from a handful of virions, where chance decides. I simulated the first days of HIV infection with exact stochastic simulation and stochastic differential equations to measure how often an exposure dies out, and how much a neutralizing antibody lowers the odds.

The question

The standard model of HIV in the body is a set of ordinary differential equations: target cells get infected, infected cells make virus, virus gets cleared. Early in infection its key number is R₀, the average number of newly infected cells each infected cell produces. Estimates for HIV put R₀ around 8. In the deterministic model, any R₀ above 1 means the infection always grows.

That can't be the whole story. The chance of HIV transmission per sexual exposure is well under 1%, and about three quarters of heterosexual infections trace back to a single founder virus (Keele et al. 2008). At the start of an infection there are only a few virions, and each one is far more likely to be cleared than to infect a cell. The early phase is a game of chance, so it needs a stochastic model.

Model

I track four populations: target cells (T), infected cells in the eclipse phase before they make virus (E), productively infected cells (I), and infectious virions (V). Each arrow below is a random event that happens at the listed rate.

infection T + V → E rate β·T·V (antibody blocks a fraction φ) eclipse ends E → I rate k·E k = 1 / day cell death I → ∅ rate δ·I δ = 1 / day production I → I + V rate p·I clearance V → ∅ rate c·V c = 23 / day target cells ∅ → T, T → ∅ rate λ, d_T·T

Clearance, death and eclipse rates are standard estimates from HIV studies (Ramratnam et al. 1999; Markowitz et al. 2003). I set the virion production and infection rates so that R₀ = 8 (Ribeiro et al. 2010). A neutralizing antibody is modeled as blocking a fixed fraction φ of infection events.

Three ways to solve it

  • Exact stochastic simulation. The Gillespie algorithm draws the time to the next event and which event it is, one at a time. It is exact but slow once there are thousands of particles.
  • A stochastic differential equation. Once populations are large, each reaction fires many times per small time step, and the count of firings is close to normal. That gives the chemical Langevin equation, which I integrate with the Euler–Maruyama method. The noise term for each reaction scales with the square root of its rate.
  • A closed-form check. While target cells are plentiful, each virion's lineage is a branching process, and the probability that it dies out solves a fixed-point equation. This gives the exact answer the simulations should match.

The full simulation is a hybrid: Gillespie until the infection reaches 200 particles and infected cells, then the SDE. At 200, the chance of extinction is negligible, so nothing is lost by switching.

Results

Most single-virion exposures die out

With R₀ = 8, a single virion reaching target cells starts an infection only 14.6% of the time. The reason is the first step. A free virion is cleared at 23 per day and infects at under 5 per day, so 83% of virions are cleared before they ever infect a cell. Even an infected cell's lineage can fail if its first few progeny are cleared. Ten virions establish infection 79% of the time, and thirty make it 99% certain.

Antibodies cut risk well before they stop infection outright

An antibody that blocks infection events lowers R₀. Once it blocks more than 89.4% of events, R₀ falls below 1 and no exposure can grow. But the per-virion risk falls steeply long before that point: 2.1-fold at 50% neutralization, 8.3-fold at 80%, and 18-fold at 85%. Each blocked infection event gives the immune system's clearance another chance to win the race.

This is consistent with what the Antibody Mediated Prevention trials found for the broadly neutralizing antibody VRC01 (Corey et al. 2021). It prevented infection by viruses it neutralized potently, and did little against viruses it neutralized weakly. In this model, the difference between 80% and 90% neutralization is the difference between partial protection and complete protection.

Infections that take hold run ahead of the deterministic model

I ran single-virion exposures until 600 had established infection (that took 4,130 exposures, an establishment rate of 14.5%, matching the formula). Among those 600, infectious virus reached 1,000 per mL at a median of 5.25 days, against 6.0 days for the deterministic model started from one virion. The middle 90% ranged from 4.0 to 7.75 days.

The stochastic infections are faster because they are selected. The ones that survive are the ones that got lucky early, with infected cells that happened to release virus quickly. The deterministic model averages over lineages that mostly die, so it underestimates how fast a surviving infection grows. This matters for the window period between exposure and a positive test.

What this leaves out

  • The mucosal barrier. The model starts once virions reach target cells. Most of the protection against sexual transmission happens before that, at the mucosa.
  • Antibody dynamics. Neutralization is a fixed fraction here. In reality it depends on antibody concentration at the site of exposure, which varies with time since dosing, and on how sensitive each viral strain is.
  • Other immune responses. There are no T cell or innate responses, which is why viral load settles at a level set only by target-cell supply after the peak.
  • Burst versus continuous release. Infected cells release virions one at a time. Pearson et al. (2011) showed that burst release changes extinction probabilities, which would be a direct extension.
