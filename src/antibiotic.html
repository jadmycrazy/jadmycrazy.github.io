Dosing antibiotics with the immune system in the loop

Jad AlmajnouniIndependent project, 2026Code on GitHubPython · ODE modeling

Most dosing models treat the drug as the only thing killing bacteria. I added a simple innate immune response and a resistant subpopulation, then searched 500 dosing regimens for the least drug that clears the infection without letting resistance take hold.

The problem

A dosing regimen has to do two things. It has to kill enough bacteria to end the infection, and it has to avoid the concentration range where resistant mutants have the advantage. Sensitive bacteria die at lower concentrations than resistant ones, so a dose that is strong enough to kill the sensitive population but too weak to touch the mutants clears out the mutants' competition. Drlica called this range the mutant selection window.

The usual answer is to dose high enough to stay above the window. But the drug is not working alone. Neutrophils and macrophages kill bacteria too, and they are most effective when the bacterial population is small. Ankomah and Levin (2014) showed in models that this changes the dosing problem: the drug only has to push the population down to where the immune system can finish the job. I wanted to measure how much drug that saves, and what happens as the immune response weakens, as it does in patients on chemotherapy.

Model

Two bacterial populations grow toward a shared carrying capacity: sensitive cells S, and resistant cells R that grow 10% slower (a fitness cost). Sensitive cells mutate into resistant ones at 10-8 per division. The drug kills each population with a Hill function, with the resistant EC50 sixteen times higher. The immune system kills at a rate that saturates as bacteria multiply, so it can control a small population and is overwhelmed by a large one.

dS/dt = g·S·(1 − N/K) − E(C; EC50_S)·S − I(N)·S − mutation dR/dt = (1 − cost)·g·R·(1 − N/K) − E(C; EC50_R)·R − I(N)·R + mutation E(C) = Emax·C^h / (EC50^h + C^h) I(N) = k_imm / (1 + N/K_imm) C(t) = Σ_doses (dose/V)·exp(−k_e (t − t_i)) half-life 2 h, V = 20 L

The infection starts at 107 sensitive cells and 1 resistant cell. Without treatment it grows to 109 and stays there, so the immune response alone can't clear it. A course counts as successful if all bacteria are gone at day 7 and the resistant population never grew past 1,000 cells. I integrate with fixed-step fourth-order Runge-Kutta (0.01 h steps) and treat any population below one cell as extinct.

The parameters are illustrative. I chose them to sit in realistic ranges for a fast-growing bacterium and a concentration-dependent drug, but they are not fitted to a specific pathogen. The results that matter are the shapes and ratios, not the milligram values.

Searching regimens

I simulated every combination of 100 doses (20 to 2,000 mg) and five intervals (every 4, 6, 8, 12 or 24 hours), with and without the immune response. That is 1,000 simulations. Then, for six levels of immune strength from 100% down to 0%, I found the regimen with the least total drug that still succeeded.

Results

With a working immune response, 120 mg once a day for a week (840 mg in total) cleared the infection without resistant growth. Without the immune response, the cheapest successful regimen was 1,480 mg once a day, or 10,360 mg in total. The immune system cut the required drug by a factor of 12.

The danger zone also moved. Without immunity, every daily dose from 40 to 1,460 mg failed with resistance. The drug cleared the sensitive population, and the surviving mutants grew back. With immunity, only doses up to 100 mg failed, because once the drug knocked the total population down, the immune response killed the remaining mutants before they could expand.

The response to weaker immunity has a cliff. At 80% immune strength the cheapest regimen needed 980 mg; at 60% it needed 4,480 mg. Above the cliff, a modest dose pushes bacteria below the level where the immune response is saturated, and the immune system takes over. Below it, the immune system can't keep up even with a small population, so the drug has to do nearly all the work. In clinical terms this is an argument for dosing immunocompromised patients differently, not just a bit more.

Once-daily dosing used the least total drug at every immune level. Without immunity, the cheapest regimen given every 4 hours used 25,200 mg in the week, more than twice the once-daily amount. Because killing in this model depends on concentration, one high peak does more than several small ones. That matches the clinical move toward once-daily aminoglycoside dosing in the 1990s, which relied on the same property. It would not hold for a drug whose killing depends on time above the MIC, like a beta-lactam, and the model could test that by lowering the Hill coefficient.

What this leaves out

  • Randomness. Mutation and extinction are random events at small population sizes. A stochastic version would give the probability of resistance for each regimen, not a yes or no.
  • Real parameters. Fitting the model to time-kill curves for one drug and one organism would turn the ratios here into actual dose recommendations.
  • Toxicity. I minimized total drug as a stand-in for side effects. Real constraints are drug specific, like peak concentration limits for aminoglycosides.
  • Adaptive immunity and tissue sites. The immune term is innate and the infection is a single well-mixed compartment.
