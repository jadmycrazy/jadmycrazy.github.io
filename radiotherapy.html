How many fractions? Choosing a radiotherapy schedule with the linear-quadratic model

Jad AlmajnouniIndependent project, 2026Code on GitHubPython · LQ model

Radiation is split into daily fractions so healthy tissue can repair between doses. I wrote an optimizer that, for a fixed limit on late damage to healthy tissue, finds the number of fractions that delivers the most effective dose to the tumor. It recovers the direction of three recent shifts in clinical practice.

The problem

The standard course for many cancers used to be about 2 Gy a day, five days a week, for six to seven weeks. Over the past decade, trials have shown that some cancers do as well or better with far fewer, larger fractions. Breast cancer moved to 15 fractions and then to 5 (FAST-Forward). Prostate cancer moved to 20 fractions and then to 5 (CHHiP, PACE-B). Other cancers, like head and neck, still get many small fractions.

Why would the right schedule depend on the cancer? I wanted to see how much of the answer falls out of one simple model, with one optimization problem.

Model

The linear-quadratic (LQ) model says a dose d kills a fraction exp(−αd − βd²) of cells. Splitting the same total dose into more fractions shrinks the βd² term, which spares tissue. How much a tissue benefits from small fractions depends on its α/β ratio. Late-responding healthy tissue has a low ratio, around 3 Gy, so it benefits a lot. Many tumors have a high ratio, around 10 Gy, so they benefit little, which means fractionation spares healthy tissue more than tumor. Prostate cancer is the well-known exception, with an estimated α/β of about 1.5 Gy.

The biologically effective dose puts schedules on one scale. For n fractions of d Gy:

tumor BED = n·d·(1 + d/(α/β)_T) − D_prolif · max(0, T − T_k) healthy BED = n·(s·d)·(1 + s·d/(α/β)_N) maximize tumor BED over n = 1 … 40 subject to healthy BED ≤ 116.7 Gy₃ (35 × 2 Gy with s = 1) d ≤ 8 Gy

Here s is the sparing factor, the share of the tumor dose that reaches the nearby healthy tissue. Modern techniques like IMRT and SBRT lower s. T is the overall treatment time in days (five fractions a week, weekends off). After a kickoff time Tk of about 21 days, fast-growing tumors start repopulating, which costs about 0.9 Gy of BED per day (Withers et al. 1988; Fowler 1989).

For each n the constraint is a quadratic in d, so the largest allowed dose has a closed form. The optimizer evaluates all 40 schedules and keeps the best one. Without repopulation the problem has a known analytical answer (Mizuta et al. 2012): fewer, larger fractions win exactly when s × (α/β)T < (α/β)N. My grid search matches that boundary, which is a useful check on the code.

Results

I ran the optimizer with α/β values from the trial literature for three sites.

Prostate. With α/β below the healthy-tissue value, every step toward larger fractions helps the tumor more than it hurts healthy tissue. The optimizer pushes to the largest dose allowed (8 Gy) and lands at 6 fractions, which is close to the 5-fraction SBRT schedules now in use. The model's gain here is large, but it is also where the LQ model is least trusted, so I would read it as a direction, not a size.

Breast. With a tumor α/β of 3.5 and s of 0.9, the tumor and healthy tissue respond to fraction size almost equally, and the curve is nearly flat: 125.3 Gy at 5 fractions against 127.3 Gy at 40. The model says the schedule barely matters for tumor control, so the choice comes down to cost, convenience and acute side effects. That is the argument FAST-Forward made by showing 5 fractions were non-inferior.

Head and neck. A high α/β favors many small fractions, but repopulation punishes long courses. The optimum is 16 fractions, the longest schedule that still ends on day 21, just before repopulation starts. It beats 35 fractions by 15% in tumor BED. That is close to the 20-fraction schedule used in the UK, and it is the logic behind accelerated radiotherapy trials.

What this leaves out

  • Acute toxicity. I only constrained late effects. Short, intense courses also cause acute reactions, which matter most in head and neck.
  • Uncertain α/β. Published estimates have wide confidence intervals. A robust version would optimize against a distribution of α/β rather than one value.
  • One sparing factor. Real plans deliver a different dose to every voxel of each organ. The next step is to replace s with dose-volume histograms.
  • The LQ model at high doses. Above roughly 8 to 10 Gy per fraction, LQ may overestimate cell kill, which is why I capped dose per fraction.
