Kidney exchange: how many transplants is an optimizer worth?

Jad AlmajnouniIndependent project, 2026Code on GitHubPython · HiGHS

A patient who needs a kidney often has a willing donor whose kidney doesn't match. Paired exchange lets those pairs swap donors in a cycle. I built a pool simulator and an integer program to measure how many transplants the cycle choice alone decides.

The problem

About a third of people who come forward as living kidney donors can't give to the person they came for, because of blood type or because the patient has antibodies against the donor's tissue. Paired exchange solves this by pooling these pairs. If donor A can give to patient B and donor B can give to patient A, the two pairs swap. Longer cycles work the same way: A gives to B, B to C, C back to A.

Every operation in a cycle has to happen at the same time, so that no donor backs out after their own patient has been helped. That makes long cycles hard to run, and real programs cap them at two or three pairs. Within that cap, the program still has to decide which cycles to run. A pool of 100 pairs has about 3,000 possible cycles of length three or less, and picking one cycle rules out every other cycle that shares a pair with it.

Model

Simulating a pool

I generate pools with the method from Saidman et al. (2006), which is the standard benchmark in this literature. Each patient and donor gets a blood type drawn from US frequencies (O 48%, A 34%, B 14%, AB 4%). Each patient also gets a sensitization level, measured as panel reactive antibody (PRA): low (70% of patients, 5% chance of a positive crossmatch with a given donor), medium (20%, 45%), or high (10%, 90%). A pair only enters the pool if it is incompatible. A donor can give to another pair's patient if their blood types are compatible and a random crossmatch comes back negative.

Choosing cycles

I list every cycle up to length L with a depth-first search. Then I solve the cycle formulation integer program (Roth, Sönmez and Ünver 2007; Abraham, Blum and Sandholm 2007):

maximize Σ_c |c| · x_c (transplants) subject to Σ_{c ∋ i} x_c ≤ 1 for every pair i (each pair in at most one cycle) x_c ∈ {0, 1}

I solve it with HiGHS, an open-source MILP solver. Even the largest pools here (200 pairs, about 24,000 candidate cycles) solve to proven optimality in a few seconds.

The baseline is a first-come matcher. It walks through the same cycles in random order and accepts any cycle whose pairs are still free. This is roughly how an exchange behaves without an optimizer: it takes good options as it finds them, without asking what each choice blocks.

Results

In pools of 100 pairs, the optimal 3-way solution matched 55.4% of pairs on average. The first-come matcher, choosing from the same set of cycles, matched 42.6%. That is 13 more transplants per 100 pairs, a 30% increase, with no new donors. Restricting the optimizer to 2-way swaps gave 45.3%, so most of the 3-way benefit only appears when an optimizer is choosing.

The first-come matcher barely improves as the pool grows. It goes from 34% at 40 pairs to 45% at 200, while the optimizer reaches 59%. Bigger pools create more possible cycles, but a matcher that takes cycles in arrival order uses up the flexible pairs early, which leaves the hard-to-match pairs with nobody.

Who gets left behind

Blood type decides most of who gets matched. A type O patient can only receive from an O donor, and O donors are in demand from every other type. So O patients pile up in the pool: they made up 60% of the patients in my simulated pools of 100, compared with 48% of the general population. Even with an optimal match, only a third of them received a kidney.

Sensitization behaved less intuitively. Highly sensitized patients were matched at 66%, higher than low-PRA patients at 47%. This is a selection effect. A low-PRA patient usually ends up in the pool because of blood type incompatibility, which often means an O patient. A highly sensitized patient is usually there because of a positive crossmatch, often with a blood-compatible donor who is easy to place. Comparing PRA levels within one blood type would separate the two effects, and it is the next thing I would run.

What this leaves out

  • Chains. Real exchanges get many of their transplants from chains started by a non-directed (altruistic) donor. Chains can be long because they don't need to happen at once. Adding them would likely raise every curve.
  • Time. Real pools change week to week. A dynamic model would ask whether to match now or wait for a better pool, which is a harder problem than the static one here.
  • Priorities. The objective counts every transplant equally. Real programs weight hard-to-match patients and medical urgency, which would change who the optimizer picks.
  • Failed crossmatches. Some planned transplants fall through at final testing. Robust formulations plan for that.
